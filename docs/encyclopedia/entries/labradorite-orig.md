### Formation

Labradorite is a plagioclase feldspar composed primarily of calcium-sodium aluminum silicate. It crystallizes in the triclinic system and commonly forms in igneous rocks. Its body color is often gray to dark gray, although individual specimens may vary in tone and transparency.

### Common Localities
- Canada (Labrador, namesake deposits)
- Madagascar
- Mexico
- Russia
- Finland (Spectrolite variety)
- United States

### Optical Character & Care

Its signature optical effect, labradorescence, occurs when light reflects and scatters through microscopic internal layers. This produces shifting flashes that may appear blue, green, gold, orange, or violet depending on the angle of view. With a Mohs hardness of 6–6.5, Labradorite is durable enough for many uses but should still be protected from sharp impact and abrasion. Clean it gently with a soft cloth and store it separately from harder minerals that could scratch its surface.

## Collector Notes

### Named for Labrador
Labradorite was identified in Labrador, Canada, in the late eighteenth century. The locality gave the mineral its name and remains central to its historical identity.

### Labradorescence
The stone's famous color play is not produced by surface pigment. It comes from light interacting with microscopic internal layers, creating flashes that shift as the viewing angle changes.

### Finnish Spectrolite
Spectrolite is a Finnish variety of Labradorite valued for especially vivid and broad-ranging color flashes. Its visual intensity makes it particularly recognizable among collectors.

### Gray Beneath the Flash
Many specimens appear predominantly gray until they are tilted toward the light. The contrast between the subdued body color and sudden iridescence is one of Labradorite's defining features.

## Historical and Cultural Verification

- Labradorite was first formally identified in Labrador, Canada, in the 1770s by Moravian missionaries; the locality name became the mineral name. This is geologically documented and confirmed via Mindat.org and Schumann.
- Inuit oral tradition associates Labradorite with the Northern Lights; the legend holds that an Inuit warrior struck the stone and freed some of the light trapped within it, while the rest remained in the rock. This legend is cited in Crystal Bible and appears across A and B sources but originates in oral tradition rather than published academic ethnography. It has been retained in Collector Notes only in the form of its mineralogical identity (the Labrador locality), without repeating the legend as established fact in public copy.
- Finnish Spectrolite was named and commercially recognized in the mid-twentieth century; confirmed via gemological sources.
- Claims connecting Labradorite to ancient Egyptian, Norse, or other specific cultural ritual uses were not found in verifiable sources and were omitted.

## Treatments, Trade Names, Imitations, and Care

- **Treatments:** Treatments for Labradorite are not documented in the gemological sources consulted. Labradorescence is a natural optical effect produced by Labradorite's internal layer structure.
- **Trade names:** Spectrolite is the accepted trade name for high-quality Finnish Labradorite displaying vivid, broad-spectrum labradorescence. Rainbow Moonstone is a widely used trade name for white or near-colorless Labradorite that displays adularescence; it is not true Moonstone (orthoclase feldspar). Both trade names are in common use in the gem and crystal market.
- **Imitations:** Glass imitations exist and may be sold as Labradorite. Glass lacks the internal layer structure that produces genuine labradorescence; a loupe or microscope will reveal the absence of natural internal features. Synthetic feldspar is not commercially significant.
- **Care:** Labradorite has perfect cleavage in two directions, making it susceptible to fracture from sharp impact. Avoid ultrasonic and steam cleaners, which can cause cleavage-plane separation. Clean with a soft cloth and mild soap in lukewarm water. Store separately from harder stones that could abrade its surface.
