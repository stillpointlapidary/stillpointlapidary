## Overview

Amethyst is the purple variety of crystalline quartz, composed of silicon dioxide and colored by iron-related defects altered through natural irradiation. It commonly forms in geodes, volcanic cavities, and hydrothermal veins. Color ranges from pale lilac to deep reddish purple and may be uneven or concentrated near crystal tips. Heat can lighten or transform the color, and much commercial citrine begins as heated Amethyst.

Amethyst is the stone of calm, protection, purification, intuition, and spiritual awareness. Its Third Eye and Crown emphasis gives it a contemplative identity centered on reflection and discernment rather than prediction. Its protective tradition centers on maintaining clarity, steadiness, and intentional boundaries in demanding settings.

### Formation

Amethyst forms as quartz crystals in volcanic cavities, geodes, pegmatites, and hydrothermal veins. Iron-related defects incorporated into the quartz lattice are altered by natural irradiation, producing its purple color.

### Collector Context

Look for attractive color, good transparency, intact points, pleasing zoning, and crystal form suited to the specimen. Deep color can be desirable, but overly dark material may obscure clarity and internal features.

Purple glass, synthetic quartz, and treated pale quartz may resemble natural Amethyst. Color zoning, natural inclusions, crystal growth features, and reliable provenance are more useful than color alone.

Amethyst is widely available in tumbles, clusters, geodes, carvings, beads, and faceted stones. Fine color, strong crystal form, large intact specimens, unusual zoning, locality provenance, and high-quality cutting can increase value.

## Collector & Curiosity Notes

### Meaning of the Name
The name comes from the Greek *amethystos*, meaning “not intoxicated.” It reflects an ancient belief about protection from excess, not a modern treatment claim.

### Once Highly Prized
Before major South American deposits expanded supply, fine Amethyst could command prices comparable to more expensive colored gemstones.

### Ancient Carved Gems
Its durability and color made it suitable for beads, seals, rings, and engraved gems across Greek, Roman, Egyptian, and Near Eastern traditions.

### Heat-Treated Citrine
Much golden-orange citrine on the market began as Amethyst. Heating changes the color centers while the material remains quartz.

## Care & Cleansing

### Cleaning
Wash with mild soap, lukewarm water, and a soft cloth or brush; dry thoroughly.

### Water
Brief water contact is safe for untreated quartz. Avoid prolonged soaking when glue, fillers, coatings, or attached matrix are present.

### Light & Heat
Avoid sustained heat and prolonged intense light, which may fade or alter purple color in some specimens.

### Storage
Store separately or wrap points and edges; quartz can scratch softer stones and may chip under sharp impact.
