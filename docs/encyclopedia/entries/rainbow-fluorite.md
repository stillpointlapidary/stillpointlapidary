## Overview

Rainbow Fluorite is the stone of mental organization, useful when information is plentiful but hierarchy, sequence, or meaning is missing. Its clarity is not merely analytical; it helps intuition and evidence meet on workable terms, making it a natural companion for study, planning, pattern recognition, and complicated decisions. Rather than promising instant certainty, it encourages a disciplined question: what belongs together, what is noise, and what needs to happen next? Its multicolored presence reflects integration without requiring every input to carry equal weight.

A banded color form of Fluorite, this calcium fluoride mineral commonly combines purple, green, blue, yellow, and clear growth zones within a single specimen. It forms in hydrothermal veins and cavities, often beside Quartz, Calcite, Barite, and metallic ores. Its cubic crystal system and perfect octahedral cleavage can create striking geometric forms, but that same cleavage makes polished towers and crystals more fragile than their solid appearance suggests. “Rainbow Fluorite” is a descriptive trade term, not a separate mineral species.

### Formation

Fluorite commonly crystallizes from hydrothermal fluids moving through fractures, veins, and cavities, particularly in deposits associated with lead, zinc, and other metallic ores. Changing trace chemistry, structural defects, and natural radiation during growth can produce distinct color zones. Cubes, octahedra, interpenetrant twins, and massive banded material all occur.

### Quality Indicators

Desirable Rainbow Fluorite shows attractive color contrast, visible zoning, good transparency for its form, strong luster, and minimal cleavage bruising. Crystals are judged by form, condition, color, locality, and associations, while towers and carvings depend on polish, orientation, pattern, and structural integrity. Vivid seller lighting should not substitute for ordinary-light evaluation.

### Identification

Fluorite is softer than Quartz and can be scratched more easily, while its perfect octahedral cleavage may create flat internal planes or geometric fragments. Glass imitations may contain bubbles or lack natural cleavage, and dyed material may show color concentrated in fractures. Repairs are common in large crystals and carved pieces, so inspect edges, corners, glue lines, and suspiciously concealed bases. The rainbow name refers only to multicolor zoning.

### Locality Variations

Chinese deposits supply abundant banded carving rough and collectible cubes in purple, green, blue, and mixed colors. Mexico is known for vivid crystals and strong fluorescence from certain districts, while England’s Derbyshire Fluorite has historic importance and includes the banded Blue John variety. Illinois and other US districts produced classic crystals now prized by collectors.

### Physical Handling

Fluorite feels solid but can split cleanly from a single impact along hidden cleavage planes. Towers, cubes, and octahedra should be lifted rather than slid, with special care around corners, healed breaks, and internal planes that reach the surface.

## Collector & Curiosity Notes

### It can break into octahedra
- Icon: geology
- Body: Fluorite’s perfect cleavage follows four directions, allowing fragments to separate into geometric octahedral shapes that can look intentionally cut.

### Fluorescence borrowed its name
- Icon: tradition
- Body: The term fluorescence was named after Fluorite, although the strength and color of the glow vary widely and some specimens barely respond.

### Bands record changing growth
- Icon: bookmark
- Body: Purple, green, blue, yellow, and clear zones preserve shifts in trace chemistry, defects, and radiation conditions as the crystal developed.

### Color does not create a new species
- Icon: magnifier-gem
- Body: Rainbow Fluorite is still Fluorite; the trade name describes multicolored zoning rather than a distinct mineral variety with separate chemistry.

## Market & Buying Notes

Rainbow Fluorite is widely available as towers, freeforms, carvings, and crystals, but cleavage damage, repairs, dyeing, and exaggerated lighting require attention. Strong banding, saturation, transparency, crystal form, fluorescence, locality, and condition influence value. Ask for ordinary-light photographs, inspect corners and bases for glue, and remember that pale zoning may appear far subtler in person. Collector-grade locality specimens and mass-market carved banded material occupy very different markets.

## Care & Cleaning

### Cleaning
Dust or wipe gently and use mild soap with lukewarm water only when necessary, avoiding steam, ultrasonic cleaning, and harsh chemicals.

### Water
Brief cleaning is acceptable, but avoid soaking, especially when fractures, repairs, coatings, or matrix are present.

### Light & Heat
Avoid prolonged intense sunlight, high heat, and sudden temperature changes that may fade color or exploit cleavage.

### Storage
Pad and isolate carefully because a drop or knock can cleave the stone even when the surface is not scratched.
