## ◼ SECTION D — OVERVIEW

### Overview

Citrine is the yellow to amber variety of crystalline quartz, composed of silicon dioxide and colored by iron-related color centers. Natural material is usually pale and relatively uncommon, while much of the deeper golden and orange material on the market is heat-treated amethyst. Both are quartz, though their color origin, zoning, and typical appearance may differ. Transparent pieces can show internal veils, fractures, or color zoning that give individual specimens their character.

Traditionally associated with abundance, confidence, motivation, and self-expression, it is often linked with Solar Plexus energy and the outward movement of personal will. Its Merchant’s Stone reputation reflects a long-standing role in prosperity and business-oriented crystal traditions. Here, manifestation is framed as directed effort supported by confidence and clarity, not as a promise of material results. Heat-treated material remains genuine quartz, but treatment should be disclosed honestly.

## ◼ SECTION G — MINERAL PROFILE

### Mineral Profile

This macrocrystalline variety of quartz receives its yellow color from iron-related color centers. It occurs naturally in igneous and metamorphic rocks and in hydrothermal veins. Most commercial material is produced by heating amethyst, which shifts its color from purple to yellow-orange. Deep orange-red material sold as Madeira Citrine is usually heat-treated and valued for saturation rather than natural color origin.

Look for lively, even color, good transparency, and crystal surfaces or cutting that show the material clearly. Most material on the market is heat-treated amethyst, which is accepted and generally affordable, but sellers should disclose treatment rather than price it as rare natural-color quartz. Visible damage, heavy color zoning, and misleading claims should weigh against a premium price.

This yellow-to-orange quartz has a Mohs hardness of 7 and is sometimes confused with yellow topaz, glass, or other golden stones. Topaz is harder, heavier, and usually more expensive, while glass may show rounded wear, bubbles, or a less natural-looking surface. Color alone cannot reliably prove that a specimen is untreated.

Commercial examples are widely available because much of the material is produced by heating amethyst, so large polished pieces, carvings, and faceted stones can remain relatively affordable. Natural-color crystals are much less common, with collectible material appearing from localities including Zambia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Madagascar, and Brazil. Confirmed natural color, strong form, clarity, condition, and documented provenance can raise the price substantially.

## ◼ SECTION H — COLLECTOR & CURIOSITY NOTES

### Collector & Curiosity Notes

#### The Merchant’s Stone
The enduring association with commerce and prosperity earned it the popular name Merchant’s Stone. The label belongs to modern crystal tradition rather than mineralogy, but it captures the stone’s long-standing use in shops, offices, and financial rituals.

#### Most Commercial Citrine Is Treated
Natural pale yellow material is relatively uncommon. Much of the gold and orange material sold commercially begins as amethyst and is heated to alter its color centers.

#### Madeira Citrine
Madeira Citrine is a trade name for deeply saturated orange to reddish-orange quartz, usually produced by heating dark amethyst. The name refers to the color of Madeira wine, not a geographic source, and finer examples often command higher prices.

#### Natural and Heated Material
Natural and heat-treated material are both quartz, but they reach their color through different processes. Natural pieces are often paler and more evenly zoned, while heated amethyst may show stronger orange tones and concentrated color near crystal tips.

## Treatments, Trade Names, Imitations, and Care

- Most commercial citrine is produced by heating amethyst; heat-treated amethyst is treated natural quartz, not an imitation.
- Madeira Citrine is a trade name for deeply saturated orange to reddish-orange material, usually heat-treated.
- Yellow glass, yellow topaz, and synthetic quartz may be confused with Citrine.
- Clean with mild soap and lukewarm water; avoid sustained heat, steam, harsh chemicals, and prolonged intense light.
