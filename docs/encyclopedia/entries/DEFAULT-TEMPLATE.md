## Overview

Clear Quartz is the colorless to milky-white form of crystalline quartz, a silicon-dioxide mineral that commonly develops as six-sided prisms with pyramidal terminations. It occurs in igneous, metamorphic, and sedimentary settings worldwide, and transparent material is traditionally called rock crystal. Internal veils, fractures, fluid inclusions, mineral inclusions, and growth features can alter its appearance without changing its mineral identity. Natural quartz may also be synthetically grown or altered with coatings and other decorative treatments.

Its crystal traditions center on amplification, clarity, and intention. Clear Quartz is treated less as a stone with one narrow emotional purpose than as an adaptable focal point that can stand alone or work beside more specialized stones. Still Point frames its reputation for “programming” as a practice of sustained attention rather than literal memory storage: the stone becomes a visible anchor for the direction, quality, or ritual purpose a person chooses to reinforce.

## Mineral Profile

Clear Quartz is crystalline quartz composed of silicon dioxide, SiO₂. It crystallizes in the trigonal system and commonly forms hexagonal-looking prisms terminated by pyramidal faces. Quartz appears in an enormous range of geological environments, including granitic pegmatites, hydrothermal veins, metamorphic rocks, sedimentary deposits, and cavities where well-formed crystals can develop.

Quartz has a Mohs hardness of 7, no true cleavage, and a conchoidal fracture. Transparent pieces may contain veils, fractures, phantoms, fluid inclusions, mineral inclusions, or rainbow-producing internal features. These can make a specimen more collectible without changing its identity. Clear Quartz can also be grown synthetically for industrial and gem use, and natural pieces may be coated or otherwise treated for decorative effects.

Quartz is piezoelectric, meaning mechanical pressure can produce a measurable electrical charge across the crystal. This physical property helped make quartz essential in clocks, watches, radios, oscillators, sensors, and electronic frequency control. It should not be used as scientific proof of metaphysical amplification. Clean with mild soap, lukewarm water, and a soft cloth. Protect points and edges from sharp impact, strong heat, and harsh chemicals.

## Collector & Curiosity Notes

### Rock Crystal
Transparent colorless quartz is traditionally called rock crystal. The term distinguishes clear crystalline material from milky quartz and from colored quartz varieties such as Amethyst, Citrine, Smoky Quartz, and Rose Quartz.

### Quartz in Technology
Pierre and Paul-Jacques Curie documented piezoelectricity in quartz and other crystals in 1880. Quartz’s stable electrical response later became foundational to clocks, watches, radios, pressure instruments, and modern frequency-control devices.

### Crystal Skull Claims
Quartz crystal skulls were widely promoted as pre-Columbian artifacts, but studied examples lack secure archaeological provenance. Smithsonian research indicates that known specimens were manufactured in the nineteenth century or later using comparatively modern lapidary tools.

### Ancient Ice Belief
The Greek word *krystallos* referred to ice, and some classical writers believed rock crystal was water frozen so completely that it could never melt. The belief reflects its icy transparency rather than geological understanding.

## Treatments, Trade Names, Imitations, and Care

- Rock crystal is a traditional name for transparent colorless quartz.
- Natural Clear Quartz may contain veils, fractures, mineral inclusions, phantoms, or fluid inclusions.
- Synthetic quartz is widely produced for technological and some gem uses.
- Decorative quartz may be coated or altered, particularly material sold under modern trade names such as aura quartz.
- Glass can imitate transparent quartz; bubbles, mold seams, uniformity, and differing optical behavior may help distinguish it.
- Mild soap, lukewarm water, and a soft cloth are suitable for routine cleaning.
- Strong impacts may chip points and edges despite quartz’s hardness.
- Sustained high heat and harsh chemical cleaners should be avoided.
