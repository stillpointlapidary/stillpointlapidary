## Overview

Selenite is a transparent crystalline habit of gypsum, the hydrous calcium sulfate mineral CaSO₄·2H₂O. Most commercial wands, towers, and plates sold under the name are actually satin spar, the fibrous chatoyant habit of the same mineral species. Both are gypsum, but their structures and appearances differ. The retail use of “selenite” for both forms is widespread and worth explaining rather than treating as a simple error.

Crystal traditions associate gypsum sold as Selenite with purification, clarity, peace, meditation, and spiritual awareness. Its Crown emphasis gives the stone a light, elevated identity, while the Third Eye adds quiet perception. Cleansing language is framed here as a symbolic and practitioner-based tradition, not a literal mechanism or scientific property.

### Formation

Gypsum forms when mineral-rich water evaporates and deposits calcium sulfate dihydrate, most commonly in evaporite basins. It may also crystallize in hydrothermal veins, caves, and clay-rich sedimentary environments as transparent selenite or fibrous satin spar.

### Collector Context

Look for intact surfaces, an attractive silky glow or transparency, clean shaping, and minimal bruising. Because gypsum is very soft, scratches and chipped edges are common and should affect value.

Satin spar, true sheet selenite, alabaster, calcite, acrylic, and plastic may be confused in the market. Habit, hardness, texture, and provenance help clarify identity, while destructive testing should be avoided on finished pieces.

Commercial satin spar is widely available and affordable in wands, towers, plates, bowls, and carvings. True transparent selenite crystals, notable locality specimens, unusual size, clarity, or provenance may command more collector interest.

## Collector & Curiosity Notes

### Named for the Moon
The name comes from the Greek *selene*, meaning moon, reflecting the material’s luminous and translucent appearance.

### Satin Spar, Not True Selenite
Most commercial wands, towers, and plates are fibrous satin spar rather than transparent sheet selenite. Both are gypsum.

### Naica’s Giant Crystals
The Cave of Crystals in Naica, Mexico, contains enormous gypsum crystals formed under rare hydrothermal conditions.

### Unusually Soft
With a Mohs hardness of 2, gypsum scratches easily and can even be marked by a fingernail.

## Care & Cleansing

### Cleaning
Use a dry or barely damp soft cloth; do not scrub, soak, or use chemical cleaners.

### Water
Avoid soaking or submerging. Gypsum is slightly soluble and prolonged moisture can damage the surface.

### Light & Heat
Ordinary light is generally safe. Avoid sustained heat, steam, and humid storage conditions.

### Storage
Store separately from harder minerals and protect edges and surfaces from pressure, abrasion, and impact.
