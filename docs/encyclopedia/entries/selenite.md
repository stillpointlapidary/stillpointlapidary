## Overview

Selenite is a variety of gypsum, the hydrous calcium sulfate mineral (CaSO4·2H2O), and most pieces sold commercially under the selenite name are actually satin spar, the fibrous, chatoyant gypsum variety that polishes into wands, towers, and plates. True selenite in the strict mineralogical sense is the transparent sheeted form. Both are gypsum, sharing the same mineral species and composition, but they differ in internal structure and visual character. The retail crystal market uses the selenite name for both, and this distinction is worth understanding rather than treating as a naming error.

Selenite's metaphysical traditions center on purification, clarity, and a peaceful quality, and it is traditionally used to cleanse other stones and clear energetic buildup from spaces, tools, and the body's energy field. It is consistently associated with the Crown Chakra and often described as a bridge between daily awareness and higher clarity. People reach for it during meditation, at the start or close of energetic practices, and whenever a lighter, more transparent atmosphere would feel welcome.

## Mineral Profile

Selenite is a variety of gypsum, the mineral calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O), forming in the monoclinic crystal system. It develops in evaporite deposits created by the slow evaporation of mineral-rich water, as well as in hydrothermal veins, cave environments, and as a secondary mineral in clay beds. Most pieces available commercially are satin spar, the fibrous gypsum variety valued for its silky chatoyance and ease of working into polished forms. True sheet selenite and fibrous satin spar are both gypsum and share the same chemistry; they differ in crystal habit and optical character.

Gypsum has a Mohs hardness of 2, making it among the softest minerals in common use. It is water-sensitive and slightly soluble, so it should not be soaked, submerged, or stored in persistently humid environments, which can soften or damage surface detail over time. Store separately from harder minerals that could scratch it. Clean with a dry or barely damp soft cloth only; avoid water, gem-cleaning solutions, and ultrasonic or steam cleaners.

## Collector Notes

### Named for the Moon
The name selenite comes from the Greek selene, meaning moon. Its luminous, translucent character made the connection intuitive across many eras. The name has persisted through centuries of use, even as polished satin spar displaced true sheet selenite as the most common commercial form.

### Satin Spar, Not True Selenite
Most commercial wands, towers, and plates sold as selenite are satin spar, the fibrous variety of gypsum with a silky layered texture and glowing chatoyance. True selenite in the strict sense is the transparent sheeted form. Both are gypsum; understanding the distinction helps collectors know what they are actually working with.

### The Naica Cave Crystals
The Cave of Swords and Cave of Crystals in Naica, Mexico, contain some of the largest known selenite formations on Earth. The translucent gypsum beams, some exceeding ten meters in length, formed over hundreds of thousands of years in hot, mineral-rich groundwater under conditions rarely found elsewhere.

### Unusually Soft for a Widely Kept Stone
Gypsum's hardness of 2 and its water sensitivity make selenite fragile in ways that less familiar minerals are not. Prolonged humidity or contact with water can soften, dissolve, or crumble even large polished pieces over time. This is worth knowing before placing selenite in environments where most crystals are comfortable.

## Historical and Cultural Verification

- The name origin from Greek selene (moon) is documented in gemological and mineralogical sources and widely confirmed.
- The Naica Cave of Crystals formation is documented in geological literature and confirmed by scientific sources as genuine gypsum selenite formed in hydrothermal conditions.
- The satin spar / selenite naming confusion in the retail market is a documented and widely acknowledged issue in gemological references. It is not a new or disputed observation.
- Ancient Egyptian uses of alabaster (a gypsum variety) are well documented but are presented as being about alabaster, not the fiber/sheet varieties sold as selenite today. The connection was not included in public copy as it relates to a different form of the mineral.

## Treatments, Trade Names, Imitations, and Care

- **Treatments:** Selenite is occasionally oiled. Satin spar wands and plates are cut and polished from fibrous gypsum; this is mechanical shaping, not chemical treatment. No dyeing or irradiation is typical for standard market pieces, though carved or colored pieces may be found.
- **Trade names:** Satin Spar (fibrous chatoyant gypsum variety, most commonly sold as selenite); Desert Rose (rosette-forming gypsum with sand inclusions, a catalog entry in this collection); Alabaster (massive granular gypsum, used primarily in sculpture and architecture).
- **Imitations:** Clear calcite (Iceland spar) may be confused with true sheet selenite. Acrylic or plastic pieces are occasionally sold as selenite, particularly in very white, uniform wands. Selenite's water solubility and hardness of 2 help distinguish it from calcite; true hardness testing is diagnostic.
- **Care:** Water-sensitive and slightly soluble; do not soak or submerge. Mohs 2 means it scratches very easily, including from fingernails. Store separately from all harder minerals. Handle gently. Clean with a dry or barely damp soft cloth only. Not suitable for water cleansing, steam, ultrasonic, or chemical cleaning.
