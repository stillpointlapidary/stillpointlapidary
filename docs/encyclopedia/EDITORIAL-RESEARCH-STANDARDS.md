# Editorial and Research Standards v2.0

---

## Voice — Global

- Calm, clear, authoritative but never clinical.
- Write for a curious, intelligent adult who is not an expert.
- Prefer direct, active phrasing over passive or hedged constructions.
- Use metaphysical language only when common, useful, and source-supported.
- Prefer: `associated with`, `traditionally used`, `often chosen`, `commonly linked`.
- Do not present metaphysical claims as measured scientific mechanisms.
- Do not name sources in public copy.
- Do not use `magic`, guru language, promotional superlatives, or em dashes anywhere in public copy, including examples, reasons, and comparison notes.
- The goal on every page: reader finishes and wants to read the next stone.

---

## Medical Boundaries

Do not claim stones diagnose, treat, cure, prevent, detoxify, or heal medical or psychological conditions.
Do not make addiction, anxiety-treatment, or sleep-treatment claims.

---

## Section-by-Section Voice and Job Definitions

Every section has a defined job and a defined voice register. Writing that does not serve the section's job is a failure even if factually correct.

---

### Hero — Signature Line

**Job:** One-line identity. What is this stone, distilled.
**Voice:** Precise, memorable, slightly poetic. Not a tagline. Not a definition.
**Rule:** Should work as a standalone sentence that makes someone want to know more.

---

### Hero — Best For

**Job:** Name the primary practical use in one phrase.
**Voice:** Direct noun phrase. No full sentences.
**Example:** "Regaining stability, strengthening focus, and returning attention to practical reality"

---

### Hero — Use When

**Job:** Meet the reader where they are. Name the moment or feeling that brings someone to this stone.
**Voice:** Second person, present tense. Reads like the reader's own thought.
**Rules:**
- Never begin with "Use [stone] when" or "Reach for [stone] when." The label supplies that context. Begin directly with the circumstance.
- Target 3 lines, 4 maximum.
- **Example (wrong):** "Use Moonstone when life is shifting and you need to listen inward."
- **Example (right):** "Life is shifting and you need to listen inward rather than force an immediate answer."

---

### Hero — Affirmation

**Job:** Give the reader something to carry. A statement in their own voice.
**Voice:** First person, present tense. Simple and direct.
**Rule:** Must feel like something a real person would actually say, not a wellness poster.

---

### Overview — Paragraph 1 (Metaphysical)

**Job:** Make the reader feel something. Why do people love this stone? What is the emotional pull? This is the magazine profile opener — not a definition, not a property list.
**Voice:** Warm, specific, evocative. A great first paragraph makes the reader feel like they understand something true about this stone.
**Rules:**
- Lead with the stone's defining role and why it matters to people.
- Develop the metaphysical identity, tradition, and energetic purpose.
- Do not open with a mineral description.
- Do not write a property list dressed up as prose.
- One compelling idea developed well beats three ideas listed flatly.

---

### Overview — Paragraph 2 (Mineral)

**Job:** Make the geology interesting. Lead with the most surprising or visually striking fact. Help the reader understand what this stone actually is in a way that feels like discovery, not a textbook entry.
**Voice:** Smart friend explaining science. Clear, a little delighted by the subject.
**Rules:**
- Lead with the most interesting physical fact, not the compositional formula.
- Make the optical or physical properties feel like a story, not data.
- Cover composition, formation, appearance, treatments, trade-name distinctions.
- **Example (wrong):** "Adularescence is created by light scattering through alternating feldspar layers."
- **Example (right):** "Moonstone glows because of an optical trick happening inside the stone — light scatters between microscopically thin feldspar layers and floats back out as that signature shimmer."

---

### Energetic Themes

**Job:** Quick personality sketch of the stone. Scannable in 10 seconds. Someone reads this and feels like they understand the stone's character.
**Voice:** Direct, fragment-friendly, action-first. No throat-clearing. No full constructed sentences when a fragment does the job better.
**Rules:**
- Each description: one sentence maximum, ideally a strong fragment.
- Never open with "It," "Its tradition," "This stone," or the stone name.
- Start with the action or the effect.
- Write for someone reading at speed.
- **Example (wrong):** "Brings awareness back to the body, the present moment, and the practical conditions that require attention. Its grounding tradition helps scattered energy become more anchored and physically present."
- **Example (right):** "Draws scattered attention back to the body and what's immediately in front of you."
- **Example (wrong):** "Creates steadier footing without becoming rigid or immovable."
- **Example (right):** "Steadies without stiffening — grounded, present, responsive."

---

### Why People Reach For It

**Job:** Meet the reader where they are emotionally. Each row should feel like the page is reading them — "that's exactly why I was looking this up."
**Voice:** The reader's inner monologue. Second person implied. Specific to a feeling or situation, not a mechanism.
**Rules:**
- No metaphysical mechanism language. Not "the energy of this stone supports X."
- Name the situation or feeling first. The stone's role is implied.
- **Example (wrong):** "Chosen when quiet intuition needs room to surface beneath competing opinions, pressure, or excessive analysis."
- **Example (right):** "When everyone around you has an opinion and you've lost track of your own."
- **Example (wrong):** "Used during transitions to support patience, emotional balance, and a more receptive relationship with uncertainty."
- **Example (right):** "When you're in the middle of a change and can't yet see where it leads."

---

### Mineral Profile — Facts Table

**Job:** Clean, accurate reference data.
**Voice:** Factual. No prose flourishes.
**Rule:** Values only. No editorializing inside the table.

---

### Mineral Profile — Formation

**Job:** Explain how and where the stone forms geologically.
**Voice:** Clear, precise, scientifically grounded.
**Rules:**
- Geology only. See Formation Rule below.
- Treatment information does not belong here under any circumstances.

---

### Mineral Profile — Common Localities

**Job:** Tell the reader where this stone comes from.
**Rules:**
- Single column, abbreviated. USA not United States. DRC not Democratic Republic of Congo.
- No editorializing. No quality judgments inside the localities list.

---

### Mineral Profile — Collector's Guide (M1–M5)

Five paragraphs rendered as flowing prose under a single COLLECTOR'S GUIDE label. Each paragraph has a distinct, non-negotiable job. Do not blend them. M4 and M5 are optional — include only when there is something genuinely useful to say.

**M1 — Quality Indicators**
**Job:** Help the reader buy well. What separates an exceptional specimen from a mediocre one? Name the things that actually matter — color, clarity, optical effect, cut, origin, size, crystal habit, whatever is genuinely relevant to this stone.
**Voice:** Knowledgeable friend at the shop. Direct, a little opinionated. "What you want is..." energy.
**Rule:** When a stone comes in meaningfully distinct forms where quality criteria differ by form, open M1 by naming that complexity first. Give the reader orientation before criteria. They should understand the frame before they get the checklist.

**M2 — Identification and Confusion Stones**
**Job:** Help the reader not get fooled. What does this stone get mistaken for? What are the telltale differences? What are the known imitations, treatments, or misrepresentations in the market?
**Voice:** Same knowledgeable friend — honest, practical, slightly protective.
**Rule:** This is where treatment disclosures and authenticity warnings live. Not in Formation.

**M3 — Market and Pricing Context**
**Job:** Set expectations. Is this stone widely available or genuinely rare? What drives the price up? What is a reasonable range for entry-level versus collector quality? What should the reader know before they shop?
**Voice:** Informed and direct. Not a pricing guide — a calibration tool.

**M4 — Locality Variations (optional)**
**Job:** Tell the reader how the stone differs by source when those differences are meaningful. Color, quality, crystal habit, collector reputation. Only include when the distinctions genuinely matter.
**Voice:** Same knowledgeable friend. Specific. Not a geography lesson.
**Rule:** Omit entirely if locality differences are not meaningful or not well-supported by research. Do not pad.

**M5 — Physical Handling (optional)**
**Job:** Describe what it is like to hold this stone. Weight, feel, surface texture, tactile experience. Particularly useful for online shoppers who cannot pick it up.
**Voice:** Direct and sensory. One clear paragraph.
**Rule:** Only include when there is something genuinely worth saying — notable weight, unexpected texture, fragility at edges, distinctive surface quality. Do not invent handling notes for stones that feel unremarkable.

---

### Collector & Curiosity Notes

**Job:** The most fun section on the page. Each note should have a hook — something that makes the reader go "I didn't know that." The title does half the work. The body pays it off.
**Voice:** Best trivia at the dinner table. Warm, a little surprising, genuinely interesting. Not a footnote. Not an academic aside.
**Rules:**
- Title: sentence case, specific, leads with the interesting thing. Not a generic label.
- Body: 1-2 sentences maximum. Hook + payoff.
- Every note should earn its place — if it isn't genuinely interesting, cut it.
- **Example (wrong title):** "A Sacred Stone of India"
- **Example (right title):** "Art Nouveau's Obsession"
- **Example (wrong body):** "Moonstone has a long history in Indian jewelry and spiritual tradition, while Western Art Nouveau jewelers helped popularize its dreamlike sheen."
- **Example (right body):** "At the turn of the 20th century, Moonstone became the gemstone of the dreamers. René Lalique built entire collections around it. It was that moment's rose quartz."

---

### Care & Cleansing

**Job:** Give the reader a quick, confident heads-up on how to care for this stone. Same information as any care guide — but written like a friend telling you before you walk out the door, not a warning label.
**Voice:** Direct, warm, a little conversational. One clean sentence per category.
**Rules:**
- One sentence per care category. No hedging. No "may" or "could."
- Say the thing directly.
- **Example (wrong):** "Protect from high heat, thermal shock, and prolonged intense light; feldspar cleavage makes sudden temperature change risky."
- **Example (right):** "Keep it away from heat and direct sun — feldspar doesn't forgive temperature swings."

---

### Related Stones — Similar Energy

**Job:** Help the reader understand this stone's personality by contrast. Not "here are stones that are similar" — but "here is how this stone is distinct from others in the same family."
**Voice:** Concise, comparative. Names the shared quality and the divergence in one sentence.
**Formula:** Shared quality first + the contrast second.
**Rule:** The reason must illuminate what makes *this* stone distinct, not just list shared traits of the recommended stone.
**Example:** "Shares the intuitive, inward-facing quality — but channels it toward transformation and mystery rather than cycles and receptivity."

---

### Related Stones — Pairs Well With

**Job:** Tell the reader what this stone is missing and how the pairing fills it. Answer the question: "Why would I use both?"
**Voice:** Practical and specific. Names the gap and the complement.
**Formula:** What this stone does + what the pairing adds.
**Rule:** Explain why the pairing is useful — what the paired stone brings that completes or extends the picture. Do not open with the primary stone's name. Do not force a contrast or gap framing when the relationship is additive rather than compensatory.
**Example:** "Adds warmth and relational energy, useful when intuitive clarity needs a more outward, compassionate expression."

---

## Required Counts

- 3 Property Pills
- 1 Best For
- 1 Use When sentence
- 2 Overview paragraphs (metaphysical first, mineral second — locked)
- 5 Why People Reach For It rows
- 1 or 2 Primary Energetic Themes, evidence-supported
- 0, 1, or 2 Secondary Energetic Themes, evidence-supported
- 0, 1, or 2 Occasional Associations, evidence-supported
- M1, M2, M3 Collector's Guide paragraphs always required; M4 and M5 optional (include only when content earns its place)
- 3 or 4 Collector & Curiosity Notes
- 2 Similar Energy related stones
- 2 Pairs Well With related stones

---

## Research Minimums

**Metaphysical:**
- Standard stone: 6–10 approved sources
- Thin-source stone: 4–6 approved sources
- A+ and A sources form the core consensus
- B sources may support but not override

**Technical:**
- 2–4 qualified mineralogical, gemological, museum, scientific, or institutional sources

---

## Theme Thresholds

- Primary: A+ support or strong consensus across at least 3 reputable sources
- Secondary: meaningful agreement across at least 2
- Occasional: supported but situational
- Omit weak, conflicting, unsafe, repetitive, or idiosyncratic claims

---

## Formation Rule

The Formation field covers geology only. Treatment information does not belong in Formation.

**Include:** how the stone forms, geological environment, color origin, crystal habits, relevant physical context.
**Exclude:** treatment disclosures, market authenticity warnings, imitation notes, fraud flags.

Treatment and authenticity information belongs in Collector's Guide M2 (identification) or Collector & Curiosity Notes — not in Formation. This is a hard separation. Do not end a Formation paragraph with a treatment sentence even when the transition feels natural.

---

## Overview Paragraph Order

**Locked rule: metaphysical first, mineral second.** Applies to all 333 entries without exception.

- **P1:** Lead with the stone's name and defining role. Develop the metaphysical identity, tradition, and energetic purpose. Compelling and readable — not a property list.
- **P2:** Mineral and physical identity. Open with the most interesting physical fact. Cover composition, formation, appearance, treatments, trade-name distinctions.
- **P2 rule:** Must not open with the stone name. Open with a descriptive noun phrase. Example: "A sodium-rich aluminosilicate mineral best known for..." not "Sodalite is a sodium-rich..."

Do not revert to mineral-first order under any circumstances.

---

## Stone-Name Repetition Control

The stone name must not be repeated mechanically throughout an entry.

**Rules:**

1. Do not begin multiple consecutive paragraphs, bullets, cards, theme descriptions, or comparison notes with the stone name.
2. In the Hero "Use When" section, never write "Use [stone] when" or "Reach for [stone] when." Begin directly with the circumstance or need.
3. After the stone has been clearly identified in a section, use natural alternatives where clarity allows: it / this stone / this material / this variety / direct sentence construction with no repeated subject.
4. Keep the stone name only where needed for: the first clear identity sentence / mineralogical precision / treatment terminology / trade names / comparisons where a pronoun would be ambiguous.
5. Do not remove the name so aggressively that the prose becomes vague or awkward. The goal is natural variation, not avoidance at all costs.
6. Audit every completed entry for repeated sentence openings, especially: "[Stone] is…" / "[Stone] supports…" / "[Stone] is associated…" / "[Stone] is chosen…" / "[Stone] pairs…"
7. No more than two nearby prose units should begin with the stone name.
8. The Hero, Energetic Themes, Related Stones, Collector Notes, and Mineral Profile require a dedicated repetition pass before approval.
9. Treat excessive name repetition as an editorial failure, even when all individual sentences are factually correct.
10. Preserve concise, natural prose. Do not replace repeated names with bloated abstractions.

---

## Editing Rules

- Preserve approved copy during structural updates.
- Report factual concerns separately before changing public copy.
- Keep sections distinct. Do not blend section jobs.
- Never leave placeholders or unresolved notes.
- Every section must earn its place. If it isn't genuinely interesting or useful, revise it — don't pad it.
