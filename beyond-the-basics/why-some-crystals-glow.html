Curious Phenomena·Inside the Stone

Why Some Crystals Glow

What Ultraviolet Light Reveals

A mineral can look completely ordinary in daylight, then flare orange, green, red, blue, or violet beneath an ultraviolet lamp.

About 7 minutes

Fluorite may flash blue-violet. Willemite can turn electric green. A plain gray Yooperlite may suddenly reveal bright orange patches.

Nothing about the specimen has changed. What has changed is what we are able to see.

Some minerals absorb invisible ultraviolet energy and release part of it as visible light. The result can feel almost theatrical, but the process is physical. UV simply reveals a response that ordinary light leaves hidden.

The specimen stays the same. The available light changes what becomes visible.

The Big Idea

Ultraviolet light can reveal a visible response that ordinary light leaves hidden.

Two Effects

Fluorescence or Phosphorescence?

These two effects are easy to tell apart once you know what to watch for.

Sometimes the interesting part begins after the light is gone.
01

Fluorescence

The glow continues after the lamp is switched off. An afterglow may disappear almost immediately or remain visible for several seconds.

Specimen by Specimen

Why Does One Glow and Another Does Not?

A mineral name alone cannot predict exactly what a specimen will do under UV. Trace elements, impurities, internal structure, locality, growth conditions, and the wavelength of the lamp can all influence the response.

Trace elementsImpuritiesInternal structure LocalityGrowth conditionsLamp wavelength

Chromium helps produce red fluorescence in some rubies. Manganese can contribute orange or red fluorescence in some calcites. Even two specimens of the same mineral may react differently beneath the same lamp.

That is why the most useful collector language is not “this mineral fluoresces,” but:

This mineral can fluoresce.

The possibility belongs to the material. The actual performance belongs to the specimen.

A Second Visual Identity

Why the Glow May Be a Different Color

What a stone looks like in daylight gives you surprisingly little warning about what may happen under UV.

A green fluorite may glow blue-violet. Pale calcite may turn orange-red. Ruby can become an even more intense red. The fluorescent color does not need to resemble the color you see under ordinary light because the two appearances come from different interactions with light.

That contrast is one of the pleasures of fluorescence. A familiar specimen can have an entire second visual identity waiting in the dark.

The Lamp Matters

Longwave and Shortwave UV

Ultraviolet light covers a range of wavelengths, but collectors usually encounter two main types.

Longwave UV
Around 365 nm

Lower-energy UV with a longer wavelength; common flashlights make it an accessible starting point.

Shortwave UV
Around 254 nm

Higher-energy UV with a shorter wavelength; some minerals respond much more strongly beneath it.

A specimen may react under both, only one, or even show different colors beneath each.

Longwave lamps are convenient for casual viewing and hunting fluorescent rocks. Shortwave equipment is more specialized and requires greater protection because the radiation is more energetic. Neither wavelength is universally “better.” They simply reveal different possibilities.

Look More Closely

What UV Light Can Reveal

Fluorescence is beautiful, but UV can also be useful.

A lamp may reveal zoning within a crystal, different minerals growing together in one specimen, coatings, glue or repair lines, fracture filling, or patterns that disappear in ordinary light. Something that looks uniform on a shelf may separate into distinct glowing regions once the room goes dark.

UV does not automatically tell you what caused the difference. It tells you that a difference is there. That alone can change what you notice and what questions you ask next.

  • Zoning within a crystal
  • Different minerals growing together
  • Coatings, glue, or repair lines
  • Fracture filling and hidden patterns

A Simple Observation

Trying It at Home

A simple viewing setup does not need to be elaborate. Start with a reputable UV lamp, a dark room or viewing box, appropriate UV-blocking eyewear, and an ordinary white light for comparison.

01Look at the specimen first in normal light.
02Turn on the UV lamp and notice which areas respond.
03Switch it off and watch briefly for any afterglow.

UV Safety

Never look directly into the beam or aim it toward another person. Shortwave equipment requires additional care because it uses higher-energy UV, and children should be supervised around any UV lamp.

Closing Thought

A Second View of the Stone

Ordinary light shows you color, luster, transparency, and form. Ultraviolet light may reveal glowing zones, unexpected mineral combinations, hidden repairs, or colors that never appear in daylight.

That is what makes fluorescence so compelling. It does not replace the specimen you already know.

It reminds you that there may still be more to see.
