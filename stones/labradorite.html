Overview

Labradorite is a plagioclase feldspar celebrated as much for its optical drama as for its metaphysical depth. Named for Labrador, Canada, where large deposits were first documented in the 1770s, it is recognized in crystal tradition as one of the defining stones of transformation and inner perception: a guide for thresholds, transitions, and the work of becoming.

Its signature play of color (shifting blues, greens, golds, and occasional violets visible only when light strikes the stone at just the right angle) has anchored its metaphysical identity across cultures. That hidden brilliance, present but not always apparent, is exactly how collectors and practitioners describe its energetic character: more is going on beneath the surface than what first appears.

Formation

Labradorite crystallizes primarily in mafic igneous rocks (gabbros, basalts, and anorthosites) formed from magmas rich in calcium, sodium, aluminum, and silica. The optical layering develops slowly as the mineral cools.

Common Localities

  • Labrador, Canada (namesake deposits)
  • Finland: Spectrolite variety, intense vivid flash
  • Madagascar
  • Norway
  • United States (Oregon Sunstone related)

Labradorite belongs to the plagioclase series of the feldspar group, occupying the labradorite–bytownite compositional range. Its iridescent color play, known as labradorescence, is caused by light scattering between thin alternating layers of two feldspar compositions with slightly different refractive indices. This is a structural color effect, not pigment. The flash is real; the color is physics. That same principle of hidden brilliance revealed by the right angle of light is precisely what anchors labradorite's strongest metaphysical associations.

Collector & Curiosity Notes

Variety Note

Spectrolite is a vivid Finnish variety prized for unusually intense, full-spectrum color flash, widely considered among the finest Labradorite available.

Historical Note

Inuit peoples associated Labradorite with the frozen lights trapped in ancient coastal rocks, one origin of its longstanding connection to the northern lights.

Optical Note

Labradorescence is caused by light interference between thin internal layers. It is a structural effect, not pigment. The flash is real; the color is physics.

Care Note

Avoid prolonged direct sunlight, which can dull labradorescence over time. Clean gently with mild soap and lukewarm water; avoid ultrasonic cleaners.
